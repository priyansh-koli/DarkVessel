Guide

Find out why a vessel was reported dark

Ships must broadcast their position over AIS. Radar sees them whether they do or not. The viewer puts one radar scene next to what AIS declared at the moment it was taken, and shows, for every vessel it found, what explains it or what doesn't.

Seven-step tour

Each step opens the viewer already set up to show one behaviour. The scene is a synthetic fixture built so that each of its five vessels takes a different path through the pipeline.

  1. Read a verdict

    Select any box on the scene or row in the table. The inspector on the right says, in words, which declaration explained it, how far apart they were, and what the position rests on. Detection 2's position was interpolated between two reports. Its nearest single report sits about 900 m away, far enough on its own to call it dark.

    Open detection 2 →

  2. Switch off the azimuth correction

    Radar draws a moving ship displaced along the satellite's track. The dashed orange line shows that shift. Turn the correction off and detection 3, the fast vessel, falls out of tolerance: 4 matched / 1 dark becomes 3 matched / 2 dark.

    See detection 3 go dark →

  3. See why the dark vessel stays dark

    Detection 5 has a declaration only 170 m away, inside the 200 m tolerance. It's still dark because matching is one-to-one: that declaration fits detection 4 better (30 m). A greedy matcher that let one declaration explain two ships would hide this vessel.

    Open detection 5 →

  4. Tighten the tolerance

    At 25 m, detection 4's declaration (about 30 m off) no longer counts. A dark count always comes with the tolerance it was measured at, and the table and inspector both show it.

    Try a 25 m tolerance →

  5. Register a fixed structure

    Turbines and platforms show up on every pass. Select Register by clicking the scene, then click a dark detection. It turns structure, and no matched vessel is ever touched: structure exclusion runs after matching.

    Register one under detection 5 →

  6. Ask whether the search could have heard

    Lower the brightness threshold to 0.30 and two more contacts appear. One is reported dark; the other is shadowed, because the only vessel near it is heard once an hour, so the archive would have placed a transmitting vessel there barely a third of the time. Switch on Show reception shadows to see the water this applies to. A third contact nearby has no estimate at all and stays dark — an absence of evidence is never treated as evidence of absence.

    Now widen the max AIS gap past an hour. The same reports start covering the acquisition instant, reception rises, and the shadow lifts: one control moves both halves of the claim.

    See a shadowed contact →

  7. Look at the other side

    A dark vessel is a detection no declaration explains. The other side, below the key, counts the mirror: declarations no detection explains. Two vessels here broadcast a position the radar drew nothing at — the nearest detection to each is further away than the tolerance. On real water that is either a position that isn't true, or a hull the detector missed.

    The panel also reports apparent recall: the share of declared, detectable, in-scene vessels the radar also drew. It is an estimate of this detector's recall on this scene that needs no labels — and it is biased, which the panel says.

    Open the viewer and read the panel →

The five statuses

Matched

A declaration, placed at the acquisition instant, explains this detection within the tolerance.

Dark

AIS was searched and nothing explains it. This is a claim about the evidence searched, not a verdict about the ship.

Shadowed

AIS was searched, but this is water the archive barely hears — it would have placed a transmitting vessel here only rarely, so the silence isn't evidence.

Structure

It stands on a registered fixed position, so it isn't counted as a vessel. Only dark detections can become structures.

Unsearched

No AIS archive was supplied, so nobody tried to explain it. Missing data is never reported as dark.

Controls

Radar or SAR image

The scene opens as a radar display: range rings from the scene centre, bearings from north, and a sweep that brightens each contact as it passes. A matched vessel is drawn as a hull pointing along its AIS course, with an arrow showing where it will be in 20 seconds. A dark contact is a pulsing red diamond. We can't know its heading, so none is drawn. SAR image shows the radar pixels exactly as they are.

Contacts move. The scene is a single instant, so the display dead-reckons each contact forward from it: a matched vessel at its declared course and speed, a dark contact at an assumed slow drift, since nothing declares its velocity. A dotted track shows where each one has come from, and the clock in the corner shows time since the pass, running three times faster than real time. Classification always belongs to the moment of the pass. Pause stops the sweep and the motion, which also stay off if your system asks for reduced motion.

Every control has an i beside it: hover over it, or tab to it, for what the control means and what it changes on the radar.

Match tolerance

How far a declared position may sit from a detection and still explain it. Lower is stricter and reports more dark vessels.

Max AIS gap

How old a lone report may be and still place a vessel. Beyond it the vessel isn't placed at all, so it can't explain anything. Reports that bracket the acquisition are always interpolated.

Azimuth-shift correction

Moves each declaration to where the radar would have drawn that vessel, given its speed, before matching.

Brightness threshold

The stand-in detector's cut-off. Lower finds fainter targets. A trained detector would replace this.

Structure register

Known fixed positions, such as a turbine list. A dark detection within the same-position tolerance of one becomes a structure.

AIS reception floor

How reliably the archive must reach a place before a detection's silence there counts as evidence. Below it, a dark detection is reported shadowed instead. It is a policy, not a measurement: every row carries its own reception estimate whatever you set the floor to, and a row with no estimate is never shadowed.

Show reception shadows

Shades the water the archive hears worse than the floor, and the water nothing measured. Anything left unshaded is heard well enough for a dark claim to stand. Purely a view — nothing is re-run.

Counts, table, Share and export

Click a count card to filter the scene and table to that status. Share copies a link that reopens the exact view: every control, every registered position and the selected detection. CSV downloads the detections, AIS CSV the declarations and their verdicts, and JSON the whole run.

Glossary

The terms the viewer, the table and the inspector use, in the sense they are used here.

AISAutomatic Identification System
The transponder system ships are required to broadcast their identity, position, course and speed on. A declaration is one such report.
SARSynthetic Aperture Radar
Radar that builds a high-resolution image from a moving platform. Sentinel-1 carries one, and it sees through cloud and darkness, so a pass works at night and in weather.
Dark vessel
A vessel the radar drew that no AIS declaration explains. It is a statement about the evidence searched, not an accusation: a transponder may be off, broken, or the archive may simply not cover it.
AIS reception
How often this archive would have placed a transmitting vessel at the acquisition instant, near a given place. Estimated from how regularly the vessels the archive does hold report nearby, using the same max gap the matcher uses. Where it is low, a detection's silence is not evidence; where nothing measures it, the row says so and stays dark.
Undetected declaration
The mirror of a dark vessel: a vessel that broadcast a position inside the scene, long enough for this detector to expect, where the radar drew nothing. Either the position isn't true, or the detector missed the hull.
Apparent recall
The share of declared, detectable, in-scene vessels the radar also drew — an estimate of the detector's recall on this scene, needing no labels. Biased: vessels that declare themselves are larger and more cooperative than those that don't.
Detection
One target the detector found in the radar scene, reduced to a single point. The pipeline compares points, not boxes, because most vessels at this pixel scale are a handful of pixels across.
Declaration
A vessel's AIS position placed at the exact instant the radar image was taken, interpolated between the reports either side of it where they exist.
Match tolerance
The furthest a declaration may sit from a detection and still explain it. Every dark count is only meaningful alongside the tolerance it was measured at.
Azimuth shift
Radar reads along-track position from Doppler, so a moving vessel is drawn displaced along the satellite's ground track, in proportion to its speed towards the radar. Uncorrected, fast vessels read as dark.
Interpolated / nearest
How a declaration's position was arrived at. Interpolated means two reports bracketed the acquisition and the position between them was computed. Nearest means only one report was close enough to use, so it was taken as-is and the inspector says how stale it was.
MMSIMaritime Mobile Service Identity
The nine-digit number identifying a vessel's transponder. It is how a declaration is tied to a vessel.
Structure
A fixed installation — a turbine, a platform — that returns a radar echo on every pass. Registered positions are excluded from the vessel count, and only after matching, so a vessel AIS explains is never reclassified.
Tile / overlap
A scene is detected in overlapping windows. Each tile owns a non-overlapping core and reports only what falls inside it, so every target is claimed exactly once.
CFARConstant False Alarm Rate
The classical radar detector, which compares each pixel with the sea clutter around it rather than a fixed level. It is the baseline the trained detector is measured against — see How it works.

FAQ

Is this real satellite data?

Not yet. The published viewer runs on a synthetic scene of five vessels, built so each one exercises a different branch of the pipeline. The same code runs on a real Sentinel-1 scene and a DMA AIS day file.

The badge says "Static build". What does that mean?

The published site has no server. When it was built, the pipeline was run for every position the controls can take, and each distinct result was saved. Moving a control looks up the matching run, so it responds instantly. Run it locally for a live pipeline (see below). Results are identical.

Why does max AIS gap jump in steps on the published site?

To keep the static build small, it pre-computes a set of gap values (1 to 120 minutes) rather than every minute. The live server accepts any value.

Does "dark" mean the ship is doing something illegal?

No. It means no declaration explained the detection within the stated tolerance, from the AIS the search actually had. The AIS panel shows what was searched and what cleaning removed. The dark call rests on that.

Can I share a specific finding?

Yes. Select Share above the scene. A panel shows the link and lists what it restores: the controls, the register and the selected detection. The link is copied for you. If your browser blocks copying (some embedded previews do), the link is already selected, so press ⌘C or Ctrl+C.

Run it yourself

No credentials, weights, GPU or network needed:

pip install -e ".[web]"
darkvessel synthesise --out data/synthetic
darkvessel serve --config configs/pipeline.yaml    # live, at http://127.0.0.1:8000

To publish your own static copy, darkvessel render --config configs/pipeline.yaml --out site writes a folder any static host can serve.
